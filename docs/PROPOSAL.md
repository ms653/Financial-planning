## 2. Methodology Grounding

**Retirement modeling** — the app should implement, not just display:
- Monte Carlo simulation over thousands of randomized market-return sequences (matching ProjectionLab's approach), not a single deterministic projection — this is what correctly captures sequence-of-returns risk. The return model must be specified explicitly and validated, not assumed: decide up front whether draws come from a historical bootstrap or a parametric distribution, use **geometric** (not arithmetic) mean returns to avoid silently inflating success rates — a classic and easy-to-miss bug — and be explicit about real (inflation-adjusted) vs. nominal returns throughout. Acceptance criterion for Phase 4: reproduce a known calculator's output (e.g. ProjectionLab or Vanguard's public tool) within a reasonable tolerance on the same inputs, so we know the engine isn't quietly wrong.
- Trinity-study-informed safe withdrawal rate as a *starting* assumption, but adjustable — and **the US 4%/3.9% figure does not transfer to the UK**. UK-specific research (Blanchett, Buffenoir, Kemp & Watt, Morningstar 2016, using purely UK market data) puts a UK-realistic safe withdrawal rate at **3.0–3.5%**, sometimes cited as closer to 3.7% in more recent commentary — meaningfully lower than the US figure, because UK long-run real equity returns have historically run about 1.3–1.6 percentage points below US returns, alongside higher platform/fund charges. The app should default to a UK-calibrated rate but expose it as an editable parameter (equity allocation, horizon, target success rate), not hardcode either the US or UK number.
- Target a 85–95% simulation success rate as the "safe" bar, shown as a distribution rather than a single pass/fail.
- **UK retirement accounts and rules differ substantially from the US model** — this is not a relabeling exercise:
  - **Workplace pension / SIPP** (defined contribution) — the UK equivalent of a 401k/IRA. Funds are locked until age 55 (rising to 57 in April 2028).
  - **25% Pension Commencement Lump Sum (PCLS)** — up to 25% of a defined-contribution pot can be withdrawn tax-free, capped at a **Lump Sum Allowance of £268,275** across all pensions combined (this cap starts to bite once total pots exceed roughly £1,073,100). The remaining 75% is taxable at the marginal rate (20%/40%/45%) when drawn, whether taken as a lump sum upfront or via ad-hoc UFPLS withdrawals.
  - **State Pension** — currently £241.30/week (~£12,548/year) for 2026/27, uprated annually by the "triple lock" (highest of CPI inflation, average earnings growth, or 2.5%). This is a meaningful, near-guaranteed income floor that should be modeled as a scenario input, claimed from State Pension age — currently 66, but the rise to 67 **is already underway starting 2026** (not a future event), with a further rise toward 68 legislated for the following decade. The engine should treat State Pension age as a per-person date input rather than a fixed constant, since it depends on date of birth.
  - **ISA wrapper (Stocks & Shares ISA / Cash ISA / LISA)** — up to **£20,000/year** per person across all ISA types combined. Gains, interest, and dividends inside an ISA are **entirely tax-free, permanently** — no CGT, no dividend tax, ever, and no need to track cost-basis for tax purposes on ISA holdings. This materially simplifies modeling for ISA-held assets versus a General Investment Account (GIA). The **Lifetime ISA (LISA)** is a distinct sub-type worth modeling separately: it has its own **£4,000/year sub-limit** (counting toward, not on top of, the £20,000 total), a **25% government bonus** on contributions, and a **25% withdrawal penalty** if funds are withdrawn before age 60 for reasons other than a first home purchase — this account type needs its own constraints, not just an "ISA" label. **Forward-looking rule change to model now, not retrofit later**: per Autumn Budget 2025, from **6 April 2027** Cash ISA contributions will be capped at **£12,000/year for under-65s** (over-65s retain the full £20,000 cash allowance), within the overall £20,000 combined allowance, and Stocks & Shares/Innovative-Finance-to-Cash-ISA transfers will be barred for under-65s. This directly affects the Cash Allocation Advisor's ISA-allocation logic (Section 4), so the £20,000 combined limit needs a **dated sub-limit parameter** for the cash-ISA portion rather than being treated as a single flat number indefinitely — this is age-conditional, so it also requires a `date_of_birth` on the person record (see Section 5's data model fix). **The same reform package punctures the "tax-free permanently" claim below in one specific way**: from April 2027, interest paid on **uninvested cash held inside Stocks & Shares or Innovative Finance ISAs** will be subject to a **22% charge**, and holding 100% money-market funds inside a non-cash ISA will be barred (partial MMF holdings are unaffected). Investments themselves inside any ISA wrapper keep full tax-free treatment — this only affects cash sitting uninvested inside an S&S ISA, a common pattern for drawdown buffers — but it means the account model needs to distinguish **Cash ISA vs. Stocks & Shares ISA** as genuinely different sub-types, not one generic `isa` label (see Section 5).
  - **GIA (General Investment Account)** — any holdings outside a pension/ISA wrapper are subject to two separate taxes that need distinct rates in the model: **Capital Gains Tax** (£3,000/year tax-free allowance, then **18%/24%** depending on whether you're a basic or higher/additional-rate taxpayer) and **dividend tax** (£500/year tax-free dividend allowance, then **10.75%/35.75%+** depending on tax band). These are two different allowances and two different rate schedules — don't conflate them in the reporting logic. Unlike a pension, GIA funds are accessible at any age but get no tax shelter.
  - There is no UK direct equivalent of Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) — UK drawdown is flexible with no forced withdrawal schedule, which actually *simplifies* the modeling relative to the US case.
  - **Tax band is a derived value, not an independent input — don't store it as a static field.** Pension contributions reduce the income figures HMRC actually tests against, so a self-declared "I'm a higher-rate taxpayer" flag would silently go stale the moment contribution amounts change. Store **annual gross income per person** instead (an editable planning assumption — defined as pre-sacrifice contractual salary, the cleanest anchor point for the arithmetic below — not precise payslip data), plus per-contribution **method** (relief-at-source / net pay / salary sacrifice) and **employer contribution amount**, and derive the actual test values from those in the engine rather than a single flat "income minus contributions" subtraction.
  - **This is two genuinely different HMRC tests, not one formula — don't conflate them** (an earlier draft of this section did exactly that):
    - **Personal allowance taper** (below) uses **adjusted net income (ANI)**: relief-at-source contributions are deducted *grossed up* (÷0.8, i.e. ×1.25), while net-pay-arrangement and salary-sacrifice contributions already reduce taxable pay before ANI is calculated, so they're not subtracted a second time if `annual_gross_income` is defined as pre-sacrifice salary. ANI is also technically total taxable income (salary + dividends + savings interest + rental + benefits-in-kind), not just salary — P1 should explicitly scope to salary-only and document that as a known limitation (understates ANI for anyone with meaningful investment income) rather than silently getting it wrong.
    - **Pension annual-allowance taper** (Section 4) uses a *different* pair of figures — **threshold income** and **adjusted income** — with different rules: adjusted income **adds** pension contributions rather than subtracting them, and critically, salary sacrificed under arrangements set up on or after 9 July 2015 is **added back** for threshold income (an anti-avoidance rule) — so salary sacrifice reduces ANI for the personal-allowance test but does **not** reduce threshold income for the annual-allowance test. A single shared formula gets this backwards for exactly the high earners this feature targets. Exact HMRC worksheet mechanics for both tests should be finalized against gov.uk guidance during **Phase 4.5** implementation (the Cash Allocation Advisor, which is what actually consumes these tests) rather than fully specified here — not Phase 3, which is deliberately scoped to a flat effective-tax-rate Monte Carlo and doesn't touch this logic at all.
  - This surfaces the **personal allowance taper**: between £100,000 and £125,140 of adjusted net income, the personal allowance withdraws at £1 per £2 earned, creating an effective marginal rate around 60% on earned income in that band (dividend/savings income in the same band sees a different effective rate, since those tax rates differ and rose further from April 2026/2027 per Autumn Budget 2025 — don't hardcode "60%" as income-type-agnostic if the engine ever models non-salary income here). This band survives Autumn Budget 2025 unchanged — the personal allowance freeze was *extended* to April 2031, so £100k–£125,140 holds for the foreseeable planning horizon. Pension contributions are the standard lever to pull income back under £100k and reclaim the allowance — a genuinely high-value recommendation for the Cash Allocation Advisor (Section 4), and one only reachable if income, not a static band label, is the stored primitive.
  - **Forward-looking rule to note now**: from **April 2029**, salary-sacrificed pension contributions above £2,000/year lose their National Insurance exemption (Autumn Budget 2025). Doesn't change 2026/27 arithmetic, but any NI-savings framing the engine ever surfaces needs to be date-aware.
- Withdrawal sequencing across wrapper types (SIPP/pension vs. ISA vs. GIA) and State Pension timing is still where the real modeling complexity sits — but it's a materially different (and arguably somewhat simpler, given no RMDs and ISA's full tax exemption) problem than the US version. **Scope this down the same way as before**: P1 ships a pre-tax/pre-wrapper Monte Carlo using a flat effective-tax-rate assumption across total drawdown; wrapper-aware sequencing (draw ISA first vs. pension first, PCLS timing, CGT/dividend-allowance-aware GIA drawdown) becomes its own later phase.

Sources: [RetirementExpert.co.uk — UK Safe Withdrawal Rate 2026](https://retirementexpert.co.uk/pension-drawdown/safe-withdrawal-rate), [PoundSense — Pension Lump Sum Allowance 2026](https://www.poundsense.co.uk/blog/pension-lump-sum-allowance-2026), [TaxFly — Pension Tax-Free Lump Sum 25% Rule 2026/27](https://www.taxfly.co.uk/guides/pension-tax-free-lump-sum-25-percent), [PoundSense — Pension Triple Lock 2026](https://www.poundsense.co.uk/blog/pension-triple-lock-2026), [OakNorth — ISA Season 2026](https://oaknorth.co.uk/blog/isa-season-2026-make-most-20000-allowance/), [Wealthify — Capital Gains Tax Allowance 2026/27](https://www.wealthify.com/blog/how-much-is-the-capital-gains-tax-allowance), [UK Dividend Tax Calculator — Dividends Inside an ISA](https://ukdividendtaxcalculator.co.uk/dividend-tax-and-isa)

**Stock analysis** — "industry standard" means triangulating multiple valuation methods rather than trusting one number:
- **DCF (discounted cash flow)** — intrinsic value from projected free cash flows; most rigorous but most assumption-sensitive.
- **Relative valuation** — P/E, EV/EBITDA vs. sector peers.
- **Quality/profitability screen** — ROE, ROIC, margins.
- **Balance-sheet health** — debt/equity, free cash flow.
- A **fundamentals checklist** gates raw numbers before they're allowed into a valuation model, catching bad inputs early (the "pre-flight checklist" pattern professional analysts use).

Conviction should scale with how many of these methods agree — the app should show all four side by side per stock, not just one verdict.

## 4. Features Proposal

### P1 — Foundation
- **Household model** — a household has multiple people; each person has their own accounts; dashboards roll up to household level and drill down to individual level.
- **Accounts** — cash, GIA (general investment account/brokerage), Cash ISA, Stocks & Shares ISA, LISA, SIPP/workplace pension, property, debt. Each account carries a **tax wrapper flag** (ISA / pension / GIA / none) since that determines whether gains/dividends are taxable — this is load-bearing for both the net worth view and later CGT/dividend-allowance-aware reporting. Cash ISA, S&S ISA, and LISA are three **distinct sub-types**, not one generic "ISA" label: LISA carries its own £4,000/year sub-limit, 25% bonus, and early-withdrawal penalty; Cash ISA gets the £12,000/year under-65 sub-cap from April 2027; and S&S ISA is the one exposed to the new 22% charge on uninvested cash from the same date (see Section 2). Manual entry always supported; live sync optional per account once a UK aggregation provider is confirmed (see Section 3).
- **Net worth dashboard** — trend over time, broken down by person, by asset class, and by tax wrapper.
- **Portfolio view** — holdings, cost basis, allocation, performance vs. a benchmark (e.g. FTSE 100/250 or a global index, depending on what the household actually holds).
- **Retirement Monte Carlo engine** — editable assumptions (spending, inflation, State Pension claiming age and amount, PCLS timing, wrapper withdrawal order), success-rate output, and **basic scenario comparison scoped narrowly to retirement timing** (e.g. retire at 60 vs 65, before vs after State Pension access age) — ships in Phase 3, part of the engine itself. This is distinct from the broader "Scenario planning" feature below (house purchase, major expense, etc.), which is a separate P2 feature with its own phase, not the same thing under two names.

### P2 — Growth
- **Stock analysis workbench** — fundamentals lookup, DCF calculator, relative valuation, quality/balance-sheet checklist, watchlist. *(Phase 4)*
- **Scenario planning** — side-by-side "what-if" comparisons (early retirement, house purchase, major expense) reusing the Monte Carlo engine — broader than Phase 3's retirement-timing-only comparison above. *(Phase 4.6, after the Cash Allocation Advisor, since both build on the Phase 3 engine and Phase 4.5's account-aware logic)*
- **Reporting** — exportable household financial statement; tax-lot detail for GIA holdings specifically, tracked against the £3,000 CGT allowance and £500 dividend allowance (ISA/pension holdings need no such tracking, since they're tax-free/tax-deferred respectively). *(Phase 4.7, since GIA tax-lot reporting reuses the CGT/dividend-allowance logic built for the Cash Allocation Advisor)*
- **Cash Allocation Advisor** — "where should my next £X go" recommendation, in two parts:
  - **Contribution waterfall** — a priority-ordered decision tree over the household's actual accounts: emergency fund topped up → pension contributions raised to at least the level needed to capture the **full employer match** (i.e. claim any portion of the match not currently being captured — an instant, effectively-guaranteed return, always first) → **if a specific person's adjusted net income sits in the £100,000–£125,140 personal allowance taper zone, surface a pension-contribution recommendation to pull that person's income back under £100k** — but present it as a strong candidate to weigh, not an automatic override of the steps below. The taper rescue is a **one-off ~60% uplift on the contribution amount**, while a debt's interest rate is a **recurring annual cost** — for a card at 35% APR, clearing it can still beat the pension move over a multi-year horizon; the advisor should compare relief-rate against APR × expected payoff duration (or at minimum surface the horizon dependence) rather than presenting 60% > APR as self-evident. This step also requires the **household can actually afford the extra contribution** while still servicing any debt minimums and without raiding the emergency fund — pension contributions are irrecoverable until access age, so this precondition must be checked, not assumed. It's a strong argument in one specific case worth calling out explicitly: **parents facing the Tax-Free Childcare / 30-funded-hours cliff at exactly £100k ANI** (a hard cliff, not a taper — losing up to £2,000/child/year plus funded hours entirely) can see effective marginal rates well over 100%, making the pension move dominant even against expensive debt; the advisor should flag this case specifically when applicable, since it's the strongest version of this recommendation. → high-interest debt (credit cards, personal loans — anything with a rate that beats realistic investment returns) cleared → LISA if eligible (25% government bonus) → remaining ISA allowance → further pension contributions (tax relief, **capped by the £60,000/year annual allowance including any available carry-forward, and by 100% of relevant UK earnings** — lower if tapered for high earners via the threshold/adjusted-income test (Section 2) or restricted to £10,000 by the Money Purchase Annual Allowance for anyone who's already flexibly accessed a pension; the waterfall must not recommend contributions past these caps) → GIA. LISA eligibility is two-tier and the engine needs both: you can only **open** one between ages 18–39, but once open you can keep **contributing** (and earning the 25% bonus) up to age 50 — an under-45 cutoff would be wrong on both counts. Personal-allowance-taper status and annual-allowance-taper status are **two separate derived calculations** (ANI vs. threshold/adjusted income — see Section 2), not one shared "income minus contributions" formula, and not a static self-declared band — this is what lets the advisor react correctly when contribution amounts and methods change. Uses live account data (remaining ISA/LISA/pension annual allowance, current debt balances/rates) rather than generic advice.
  - **Debt-vs-save comparator** — for lower-rate debt where the answer isn't obvious (mortgage overpayment vs. investing being the classic UK case), don't hardcode a verdict — show the numbers side by side. This needs two distinct mortgage figures, not one: the **penalty-free overpayment allowance** (most UK mortgages allow ~10%/year of the balance without charge — the exact balance basis, original loan vs. current outstanding, varies by lender and must be checked per mortgage) and, separately, the **Early Repayment Charge rate** actually charged on any overpayment *above* that allowance — these are two different numbers and the comparator needs both to correctly cost out a lump sum that exceeds the free allowance. Also needed: expected investment return **inclusive of** (not net of — the bonus/relief add to the return, they don't subtract from it) the LISA bonus or pension tax relief where relevant. **LISA is a poor fit for anyone already comparing against mortgage overpayment**: if they already own a home, LISA funds can only be withdrawn penalty-free for a first home purchase or from age 60 — an early withdrawal for any other reason (including redirecting it toward the mortgage) incurs a 25% government withdrawal charge, which is a **net loss on the original contribution**, not just a clawback of the bonus. The comparator must model this lock-in explicitly rather than treating LISA as freely comparable liquidity. Frame the remaining trade-off as "here's the numbers," since which side wins depends on risk tolerance and current mortgage rates, not a fixed rule.
  - For **multiple debts**, support both avalanche (highest interest first — mathematically optimal) and snowball (smallest balance first — psychological wins) ordering as a user-selectable toggle, since the "right" choice is behavioral, not financial. Both orderings need the debt's **current balance** and **minimum payment**, not just its interest rate.
  - Uses the `debt_terms` fields already scoped into the Phase 1 data model (interest rate, current balance, minimum payment, overpayment allowance, ERC rate — see Section 5's data model and field-naming fix below); this feature doesn't introduce new fields, it consumes ones Phase 1 already ships.

## 5. Implementation Plan

### Tech stack
Reuse the pattern already proven in the sibling Warhammer-app repo, minus the CMS half (no editorial content here):
- **Next.js 14 App Router** — server components by default, `'use client'` only where interactive state is needed (calculators, what-if sliders). Pinned specifically to match the sibling Warhammer-app repo's patterns for reuse — by the time this is built, newer major versions exist; staying on 14 is a deliberate reuse-over-currency choice, not an oversight, and not a goal to chase the latest version.
- **Postgres**, running locally via Docker — no need for hosted Supabase; this is a single-household, local-first tool, not a multi-tenant SaaS.
- **Drizzle ORM + drizzle-kit** for schema and migrations — a real gap in earlier drafts, which named "Postgres" but never specified how 8+ phases of schema changes get applied to the household's only copy of real financial data without data loss. Chosen over Prisma because it emits plain, reviewable SQL migration files (worth being able to eyeball exactly what runs against irreplaceable data) and stays lightweight in a Docker container; the schema file is the canonical source of truth. Migrations are forward-only and numbered; the deploy script (below) takes a `pg_dump` automatically *before* running pending migrations, which is the real rollback mechanism at this scale — down-migrations are a nice-to-have, not required.
- **GitHub Actions CI** — one workflow (`tsc --noEmit`, `vitest run`, lint) on every push, no CD (there's nothing to deploy to *from* CI — deployment is the local script below). Cheap, and the point of a calculation-correctness-critical codebase is catching regressions before they reach the household's real numbers.
- **Tailwind CSS** for styling scaffolding now, refined later in the design pass.
- **Zustand** for retirement-scenario and stock-analysis-workbench **UI/input state only** (mirrors the existing army-builder store pattern) — the actual computation (Monte Carlo simulation, DCF, Cash Allocation Advisor logic) runs **server-side**, not in the browser. This is a deliberate choice, not an implementation detail: it's what gives mobile full parity **when connected** (Section 4/5 mobile access) — a phone sends inputs and receives results identically to a laptop, since it never has to run thousands of simulation iterations client-side. We accept that new computations require connectivity in exchange for a single server-side compute implementation; the offline layer below covers the disconnected case by caching last-run results rather than duplicating the compute client-side. **Boundary with the offline layer**: Zustand stays ephemeral, session-only UI state — it is not persisted and is not where offline caching happens. The offline read-cache and write-queue (below) live in IndexedDB behind their own data-access layer; components read synced/cached data through that layer, and Zustand may hydrate transient UI state from it on load, but the two are not the same store. **Correction to "via Next.js Server Actions/API routes" (an earlier draft's framing)**: see Compute execution model below — the actual mechanism is compute-persist-poll, not a blocking Server Action, since a CPU-bound simulation blocking Node's single event loop would freeze every other request from every household member for the duration, and a synchronous response held open over a flaky mobile connection risks losing the result entirely.
- **Vitest + Testing Library**, **Playwright** for E2E — same as the sibling project. See Testing strategy below: reference-calculator agreement alone is necessary but not sufficient for a tool making real retirement/investment recommendations.

### Compute execution model
An earlier draft said Monte Carlo/DCF/Cash Allocation Advisor compute runs "server-side via Next.js Server Actions/API routes" and left it there — this was under-specified to the point of being wrong. Self-hosted Next.js in a Node container has no platform-imposed timeout (that's a Vercel-specific constraint, inert on `next start`), so "timeout" isn't the real risk. The real risk is that **Node is single-threaded**: a CPU-bound simulation executing inside a request handler blocks the event loop for its entire duration, freezing every other request from every household member — including the second person's dashboard load. Separately, a synchronous response held open over a mobile Tailscale connection with variable latency can simply drop mid-flight, losing the result.

**Pattern: compute → persist → fetch, not request-response-with-result-in-body.** A request creates a `simulation_run` row (status `running`), the engine executes inside a **`worker_thread`** (off the main event loop), writes its result to that row on completion, and the client polls the row every 1–2s until it flips to `complete`. No queue infrastructure (pg-boss, BullMQ) needed at this scale — an in-process worker plus a status row gets non-blocking compute, resilience to a dropped mobile connection (reconnect and re-fetch the row), *and* doubles as the persisted artifact the offline cache (Mobile/tablet access, below) serves as "most recently computed" results. Decision rule: if a single run benchmarks under ~2s on the target laptop, a synchronous call may be acceptable for desktop-only use, but the persisted-run pattern should be used regardless, since Phase 4.6's scenario *batch* comparisons (N runs) will exceed that. Cap iterations server-side so a pathological input can't pin a core indefinitely.

The engine also needs a **seeded, deterministic RNG** — not previously specified. This is what makes bit-identical results possible for a fixed seed (needed for the property-based tests in Testing strategy, below) and makes "why did this number change" debuggable, rather than every run being silently different.

### Local-first architecture
- Runs via `docker compose up` on your laptop: one container for Postgres, one for the Next.js app.
- All financial data stays on your machine — nothing leaves except outbound calls to the chosen UK Open Banking provider (Section 3) and market-data providers (FMP/Finnhub or EODHD/Twelve Data) for **bank refresh** (pulling account balances from an external provider — distinct from **device sync**, the phone-to-backend write-queue flush described in Mobile/tablet access below; these are two different mechanisms and this document now uses distinct terms for them to avoid the ambiguity an earlier draft had).
- **Deploy runbook, not an implied `git pull`**: a `deploy.sh` (or Make target) runs `git pull` → `docker compose build` → `pg_dump` (pre-migration safety dump) → run pending Drizzle migrations → `docker compose up -d`. This is where the migration-before-start and dump-before-migration ordering from the Tech stack section actually lives — an implied deploy flow has nowhere to enforce that ordering.
- Auth: see the dedicated Security notes section below — "a single passphrase gate" is a posture, not yet a spec, and an earlier draft left it at that.
- **Backup is not optional, and an unwatched backup is not a backup**: all household financial data lives in one Postgres container on one laptop with no redundancy by default. Phase 0 must include an automated `pg_dump` on a schedule plus an encrypted copy shipped off-machine (e.g. to cloud storage, encrypted before upload), full-disk encryption on the laptop as a stated prerequisite, **a visible "last successful backup: ⟨time⟩" indicator in the app itself** (red if stale — the app is the monitoring surface its users actually look at, not a log file nobody checks), and a **quarterly restore test** (restore the dump into a scratch container) written into Phase 0's definition of done. A `pg_dump` that's been silently failing for six months is the single most likely catastrophic failure mode for this system.

### Provider abstraction
**One shared interface per data type doesn't fit all providers equally, and forcing it would make the interface lie.** Quotes/fundamentals providers (Finnhub, FMP, EODHD, TwelveData) are all stateless API-key REST calls — `getQuote(ticker): Quote` is genuinely swappable, and `lib/data-providers/{finnhub,fmp,eodhd,twelvedata}.ts` behind one shared `QuoteSource`/`FundamentalsSource` interface is exactly right for them, same fallback-gracefully pattern the Sanity client already uses in the sibling repo.

UK Open Banking does **not** fit that shape. It involves a browser redirect consent flow with a callback route, access/refresh token persistence per bank connection, token refresh with failure states, and — the part a data-fetch interface has no vocabulary for — **consent expiry (~90-day reconfirmation under UK Open Banking rules)**, a user-facing lifecycle event, not an implementation detail a `syncAccounts()` method can absorb. So the abstraction splits into two layers:
- **`ConnectionProvider`** (Open Banking only) — owns consent initiation (returns a redirect URL), the OAuth callback, token storage/refresh, and a `getConnectionStatus(): 'active' | 'expired' | 'needs_reauth' | 'error'` surface the UI renders directly.
- **`AccountDataSource`** — pure data-fetch, takes an active connection as input; sits behind the same shared-interface pattern as the quote/fundamentals sources above, so UI and calculators still only see data-source contracts and stay swappable.

This split is cheap insurance for the Phase 5 spike specifically: the hard-timeboxed spike can burn down `ConnectionProvider` risk (which provider, does their consent flow actually work for personal use) without touching the `AccountDataSource` contract that Phases 1–4.7 are built against — so a failed spike doesn't invalidate work already shipped. **Security consequence**: Open Banking refresh tokens are per-connection bearer credentials for real bank accounts, living in the *database*, not `.env.local` — they need encryption at rest (app-level AES with a key from env, or `pgcrypto`, is sufficient at this scale), which the existing "secrets in `.env.local`" line doesn't cover.

The provider name in the interface is still a placeholder, not a commitment — the account-sync provider in particular is unconfirmed pending the Phase 5 spike (Section 3), and given how fast that provider landscape has churned already, the interface needs to work with zero sync providers connected (manual-entry-only) through Phases 1–4.5 regardless of which one is eventually chosen.

### Mobile/tablet access
No separate native app. The same Next.js app is a responsive PWA (installable manifest, mobile-friendly layouts for dashboard + quick-entry views). Reached from phone/iPad via **Tailscale** back to the laptop — WireGuard-encrypted point-to-point, no public port exposed, no separate hosting bill.

**Offline capability is a real requirement, not just "wait until reconnected."** When the backend (laptop) is unreachable, access degrades to a specific tier rather than dropping to nothing:
- **Available offline (cached)**: net worth summary and trend, portfolio holdings, the most recently computed retirement plan/Monte Carlo output, the most recent stock analysis results — all read-only, served from a local cache of last-synced data, not requiring a live round trip. Each cached view carries a visible **"last synced ⟨time⟩" badge** — a days-old net worth figure shown without that label is worse than no figure at all.
- **Available offline (queued)**: manual data entry — updating a balance, logging a transaction — writes to a local store immediately (optimistic UI) and queues for **device sync**. Every queued write carries a **client-generated idempotency key**, with server-side upsert on that key — flaky reconnects mean retries after ambiguous failures, and without idempotency keys a retried write becomes a duplicate transaction, not just a design nicety.
- **Conflict handling**: last-write-wins, but not naively. CRDTs are overkill for a single household — that conclusion holds — but the real risk isn't two people editing at once, it's **delayed delivery**: a phone queues an edit while offline, a different device edits the same record before the phone reconnects, and the phone's now-stale write arrives *after* the newer one. "Last write" must therefore be keyed on the **client's edit timestamp captured at entry time**, with the server rejecting (or flagging) an incoming write older than the record's current `updated_at` — not simply "whichever write reaches the server last wins." Where possible, prefer **append-only modeling** over the conflict question entirely: transactions are naturally append-only (no conflict possible), and balance updates can be modeled as dated snapshots ("balance as of ⟨date⟩") where the latest as-of-date wins and history is retained rather than overwritten — this eliminates most of the conflict surface rather than just handling it well.
- **Requires the live backend**: anything that has to *compute* something new or fetch fresh external data — running a new Monte Carlo simulation, the DCF calculator, the Cash Allocation Advisor's recommendation, live market quotes. These are unavailable offline **by design** (see the Tech stack section's tradeoff note above) and should show an explicit "reconnect to run this" state, not fail silently or look broken.

**iOS constraints materially change the sync mechanism, not just the polish** — worth specifying now rather than discovering in Phase 6:
- **No Background Sync API on iOS Safari**, with no indication it's coming. The write queue cannot flush in the background on an iPhone; it flushes only in the **foreground on reconnect** (app opened or brought forward while online) — the spec should name this mechanism explicitly rather than assume background sync works everywhere.
- **iOS evicts script-writable storage after ~7 days of disuse for browser-tab PWAs** — this can silently wipe IndexedDB, including unsynced queued writes, for anyone using the app from a browser tab rather than installing it. Mitigations: treat **"install to home screen" as a prompted, supported path**, not optional polish (home-screen-installed PWAs are exempt from this eviction); call `navigator.storage.persist()`; and surface a **pending-queue count** in the UI so a stuck queue is visible rather than silently lost.
- **Auth expiry while offline**: cached read views must render without a live session, and a token that expires during an offline window must trigger re-authentication without dropping the queued writes.
- **Next.js specifics**: Server Actions are POST requests and cannot be cached by a service worker the way GET responses can — the offline read-cache needs its own fetch path, not a Server Action passthrough. **Serwist** (the maintained successor to next-pwa) is the candidate library. The service worker needs an explicit update/versioning strategy so users don't run a stale app shell indefinitely after a deploy.
- **Security note**: cached balances and queued entries live **unencrypted in IndexedDB on the device**. Likely acceptable behind a device passcode for a household app, but this should be a stated, conscious threat-model decision, not an omission.

**Data-provider correctness note (Phase 2), surfaced by this section's ETF-tracking work**: LSE prices are quoted in **pence (GBX/GBp), not pounds**, and providers are inconsistent about labeling this — a documented, recurring bug class in portfolio tools is GBX silently misread as GBP, producing 100x valuation errors. Vanguard ETFs also sometimes have multiple listings by currency (e.g. a GBP line and a USD line) — the Phase 2 provider verification (Open Question 5, Section 3) must confirm it's pulling the **LSE GBP line specifically** and explicitly check whether a given provider returns GBX or GBP, with a normalization step in the portfolio-valuation layer either way. This is the single most likely correctness bug in Phase 2 and costs one line to pre-empt now.

This needs a genuine offline layer — a service worker, local cache (e.g. IndexedDB) for the read-cacheable data above, and a write-queue for manual entries — not just a PWA manifest; Phase 6's scope (below) is sized accordingly.

**Known limitation, now narrower than before**: full app functionality (including all analysis/compute tools) only works while the laptop is on, awake, and reachable — but *some* functionality (viewing current state, manual entry) works regardless, per the tiers above. PWA install and service workers also require HTTPS, so the `tailscale cert` step needs to be part of setup, not assumed. If the laptop-must-be-on constraint on the compute tools specifically proves annoying in practice, the upgrade path is moving the Docker deployment to an always-on mini-PC or NAS on the home network, with the laptop and phone both as Tailscale clients — worth flagging now so it's not a surprise later, not necessarily worth building for on day one.

### Security notes
The threat model itself is right and doesn't need multi-tenant auth or MFA — Tailscale as the network boundary, passphrase as a device-loss/houseguest gate, single household. But "a single passphrase gate is sufficient" was a posture, not a spec, and Phase 0 lists auth as a deliverable — without the detail below, it gets implemented as `if (input === process.env.PASSPHRASE)` and never revisited. Minimum spec, all cheap, needed **before** Phase 0 starts:
- **Hashing**: argon2id (bcrypt cost ≥12 as fallback if native deps complicate the Docker build), constant-time verify — never a plaintext or bare-hash comparison.
- **Session mechanism**: signed, HTTP-only, `SameSite=Lax` cookie (`iron-session` or a hand-rolled HMAC token), 30-day rolling lifetime — aggressive expiry just trains passphrase-typing on a phone for a household tool. The PWA offline layer (Phase 6) needs a defined answer for what "an authenticated cached read" means without a live session, which this session design should specify.
- **Brute-force protection**: a trivial in-memory counter (5 failures → 60s lockout) — cheap, and worth having even though Tailscale means only enrolled devices can reach the port at all.
- **CSRF**: Server Actions in Next.js 14 App Router get built-in same-origin enforcement (POST-only, Origin/Host check) — worth *stating* rather than assuming, since it's easy to assume coverage that doesn't extend to the compute-persist-poll API routes (Compute execution model, above) or the Open Banking OAuth callback route, which are ordinary route handlers with no such default. `SameSite=Lax` plus an explicit origin check on mutating API routes closes that gap.
- **HTTPS pulled forward into Phase 0, not Phase 6**: an earlier draft scheduled `tailscale cert` in Phase 6 while auth shipped in Phase 0 — meaning the session cookie would travel over plain HTTP for five phases and couldn't carry the `Secure` flag, and several PWA prerequisites (service workers, `navigator.storage.persist()`) require a secure context regardless. Tailscale Serve HTTPS is a one-command feature; moving it to Phase 0 removes this whole class of problem rather than discovering it mid-build.
- Secrets: environment-only API keys (market data providers) stay in `.env.local`, never committed; **Open Banking refresh tokens are a different category** — they're per-connection bearer credentials for real bank accounts stored in the database, not env vars, and need encryption at rest (see Provider abstraction, above).
- No third-party analytics or telemetry given the sensitivity of the data.
- Passphrase + Tailscale network boundary is the security model; revisit if this ever needs to run somewhere other than your own hardware.

### Testing strategy
"Reproduce a named reference calculator within tolerance" (Phase 3's acceptance criterion, Section 2) is a necessary *calibration* gate but not sufficient test coverage for a tool making real retirement/investment recommendations — it's one point in the input space, can't be re-run once the engine grows features the reference lacks (PCLS timing, wrapper withdrawal order, UK-specific taper logic — this project's own differentiators), and agreement within tolerance can mask two compensating bugs. Four additional layers, all cheap relative to the risk of a wrong number:
- **Analytic closed-form cases**: with volatility = 0, the simulation must equal deterministic compounding exactly (to numeric tolerance) — the single highest-value test for a Monte Carlo engine, since the expected answer is independently computable. Similarly: zero spending → balance never decreases; spending exactly equal to guaranteed income → balance flat in real terms.
- **Property-based tests** (e.g. fast-check): success rate is monotonic (non-decreasing in starting balance, non-increasing in spending, non-increasing in retirement length); results are invariant to iteration order; success rate stays within [0,1]; with a fixed seed, results are bit-identical (requires the seeded RNG from Compute execution model, above).
- **Named edge cases as an explicit list, not "handle edge cases" as a vague criterion**: zero/near-zero balance; a 100% loss year; retirement age after vs. before State Pension age; PCLS taken at 55 vs. deferred; a person dying mid-plan / single-survivor spending; withdrawal order exhausting one wrapper entirely; inflation exceeding returns for the whole horizon; contributions continuing past assumed retirement.
- **Convergence check**: assert that success-rate variance across repeated runs at N iterations stays within the tolerance the UI displays, so the iteration count is a justified engineering decision, not a guess — this also sets the runtime budget that Compute execution model's synchronous-vs-persisted-run threshold depends on.

The same discipline applies to the **Cash Allocation Advisor (Phase 4.5)** — arguably higher-risk than the Monte Carlo engine, since it's rule-based UK tax logic (taper zone, annual allowance, LISA rules) with hard right answers and no reference-calculator excuse: it needs table-driven test vectors per tax rule.

**UK tax parameters change most tax years** (allowances, taper thresholds, State Pension age, LISA caps) — nothing elsewhere in this document says where they live. A versioned `tax_year_config` module (a typed constants file per tax year, no database table needed) that both the advisor and the engine consume turns the annual update into "one file plus its test vectors," not a grep across the codebase.

Playwright E2E needs its own ephemeral Postgres (a compose profile or testcontainers) with seed fixtures — otherwise E2E runs end up hitting real household data, which is both a correctness and a privacy problem.

## Open Questions — Resolved

1. **Account sync**: manual entry is fine as the primary mode. Enable Banking (Section 3) is a research candidate, not a confirmed-working integration — Phase 5 is where it actually gets tried, not assumed.
2. **Account types**: no non-UK accounts. Just ISA, pension, bank account, house — all already in scope for Phase 0–1, no changes needed to the account model.
3. **Household size**: no cap needed. `person (1..n)` was already unbounded in the data model (Section 5) — this was confirming existing design, not changing it.
4. **Mobile capability when connected, tiered offline access when not**: when the backend is reachable (laptop on, Tailscale connected), the phone is exactly as capable as a laptop for every calculation — the Monte Carlo engine, DCF calculator, and Cash Allocation Advisor all run **server-side**, so the phone never has to do the heavy computation itself; that's full parity **while connected**, a conscious tradeoff for having one compute implementation rather than duplicating it client-side. When unreachable, access doesn't drop to zero: cached read-only views (net worth, portfolio, most recent plan/analysis results, each labeled with a "last synced" time) and manual data entry (queued locally with idempotency keys, flushed on reconnect — foreground-only on iOS, which has no background sync) both keep working offline; only actions that require *new* computation or fresh external data — running a new simulation, live quotes — need the live connection (see Section 5's Mobile/tablet access for the full tiering, including iOS-specific storage/sync constraints, and Phase 6 for the resulting offline-layer scope). Touch-friendly input design for things like Monte Carlo assumption sliders is a Phase 7 (visual design pass) concern, not a missing-feature one.
5. **Portfolio mix**: mostly ETFs via Vanguard. Implications for Section 3's data-source choice: (a) **Portfolio tracking (Phase 2) mainly needs ETF price/NAV data, not deep company fundamentals** — a lighter requirement than originally scoped for, since fundamentals-heavy providers (FMP) matter more for the Stock Analysis Workbench (Phase 4) evaluating individual stock picks than for valuing an existing ETF-heavy portfolio. (b) **Vanguard ETFs bought by UK investors (e.g. VUSA, VWRL) are typically LSE-listed even though the underlying exposure is US/global** — so "mostly ETFs, not individual UK stocks" does **not** mean LSE coverage is unnecessary; it means the LSE coverage need is for a small number of ETF tickers rather than broad UK equity coverage. (c) **Verification checklist for Phase 2 kickoff, not just "check LSE coverage"**: confirm the provider returns the correct **LSE GBP line** specifically (some Vanguard ETFs have both GBP and USD-denominated listings), and confirm whether it returns prices in **pence (GBX) or pounds (GBP)** and labels which — a well-documented recurring bug in portfolio tools is GBX silently misread as GBP, a 100x valuation error (see Mobile/tablet access for the full note).

## 6. Review Notes (Fable pass)

## 9. Design Correction — Tax Band as a Derived Value

Following on from the fourth Fable pass, a follow-up conversation surfaced a design flaw not caught by any review pass: the plan implied storing "tax band" as a static per-person field. That's wrong, because pension contributions change adjusted net income, which is what determines band in the first place — a self-declared band would go stale the moment someone changes their contributions. Fixed by storing `annual_gross_income` per person instead and deriving band, personal-allowance-taper status (£100k–£125,140, ~60% effective marginal rate), and annual-allowance-taper status from income minus pension contributions at calculation time. Also added: the Cash Allocation Advisor now explicitly checks for personal-allowance-taper exposure and treats reclaiming it via pension contributions as a top-priority move, ahead of high-interest debt — since a guaranteed ~60%-marginal-rate rescue plus tax relief will typically beat clearing most debt.

## 10. Fifth Fable Pass — Verifying the Tax Band Derivation

Given a diff again (curated context), but with real room to search (~52k tokens, 6 searches) and no tight response cap, since the last few passes have been cheap enough to afford the depth. Verdict: **the derivation direction was right but the arithmetic in Section 9 shipped two real defects** — worth catching before this became a spec engineers build against.

- **Conflated two different HMRC tests.** The personal-allowance taper (uses *adjusted net income*) and the pension annual-allowance taper (uses *threshold income* and *adjusted income*) are genuinely different calculations, not one shared "income minus pension contributions" formula as Section 9 had implied. They even disagree on salary sacrifice: it reduces ANI for the personal-allowance test, but is **added back** (doesn't reduce) threshold income for the annual-allowance test, per an anti-avoidance rule for sacrifice arrangements from 9 July 2015 onward. A single formula would have told a high earner they could sacrifice their way out of the annual-allowance taper — they can't. Section 2 now specifies these as two separate calculations and defers exact worksheet mechanics to Phase 3 implementation against HMRC's own guidance, rather than fully inventing the formula here.
- **`annual_gross_income` had no defined semantics.** Relief-at-source contributions need to be grossed up (×1.25) before deducting for ANI; net-pay and salary-sacrifice contributions already reduce taxable pay before ANI exists, so subtracting them again double-counts. Fixed by defining `annual_gross_income` as pre-sacrifice contractual salary and adding a required contribution `method` field (relief-at-source / net pay / salary sacrifice) plus employer contribution amount, since the annual-allowance test needs employer contributions as an input the old model didn't capture at all.
- **ANI is technically total taxable income** (salary, dividends, savings interest, rental, benefits-in-kind), not just salary. P1 is now explicitly scoped to salary-only with that documented as a known limitation, rather than silently understating ANI for anyone with investment income.
- **The new waterfall step was overstated.** "Generally the single highest-value move" ignored that the taper rescue is a one-off ~60% uplift while debt interest is a recurring annual cost — over a long payoff horizon, clearing high-APR debt can still win. Reworded to compare relief-rate against APR × payoff duration rather than asserting the pension move always wins, and added an explicit affordability precondition (can the household actually make the contribution while servicing debt minimums, without raiding the emergency fund).
- **A stronger version of the argument was missing entirely**: the Tax-Free Childcare / 30-funded-hours **cliff** at exactly £100k ANI (not a taper — a hard loss of up to £2,000/child/year plus funded hours) can push effective marginal rates over 100% for parents of young children, making the pension move genuinely dominant in that specific case. Now called out explicitly as the strongest version of the recommendation.
- **Missing caps added**: the annual-allowance contribution cap now explicitly includes available carry-forward and the 100%-of-relevant-UK-earnings limit, neither of which the waterfall previously mentioned.
- **Forward-looking note added** (matching the doc's existing pattern for the 2027 cash ISA cap): from April 2029, salary-sacrificed pension contributions above £2,000/year lose their NIC exemption — doesn't change current arithmetic, but any future NI-savings framing needs to be date-aware.
- Confirmed correct and unchanged: the £100k–£125,140 taper band and ~60% earned-income marginal rate (Autumn Budget 2025 extended the personal allowance freeze to April 2031, so this band holds), and the £60,000 annual allowance / £10,000 MPAA figures.

## 12. Seventh Fable Pass — Mobile/Offline Architecture Deep-Dive

First pass explicitly targeted at technical implementation rather than UK tax/market research, reviewing the two most recent commits (server-side compute decision, mobile offline-model correction). ~50k tokens, 5 searches, no length cap. Verdict: **approve the direction — the tiered offline model is the textbook-correct shape — but three real engineering gaps needed patching before this becomes a spec engineers build against.**

- **The LSE-ETF claim was verified correct** (VUSA/VWRL are genuinely LSE-listed UCITS ETFs), but verifying it surfaced a real, well-documented bug class this doc had no defense against: **LSE prices are quoted in pence (GBX), not pounds, and portfolio tools routinely misread GBX as GBP for a 100x valuation error.** Added an explicit Phase 2 blocking-verification requirement — confirm the correct LSE GBP line (not a USD cross-listing) and confirm/normalize GBX vs. GBP labeling — since this is now flagged as the single most likely correctness bug in that phase.
- **Last-write-wins was the right conclusion for the wrong reason.** The doc had justified it as fine because "concurrent edits are unlikely" — but the actual risk in an offline-write-queue architecture is *delayed delivery* (a stale queued write arriving after a newer edit), which is the common case, not an edge case. Fixed by keying conflict resolution on client-side edit timestamp (not server arrival time) with server-side rejection of stale writes, and — better — preferring **append-only modeling** (transactions, dated balance snapshots) over the conflict question entirely wherever possible, since appends can't conflict at all.
- **iOS constraints were entirely unstated and they change the sync mechanism, not just the polish**: no Background Sync API on iOS Safari (write queue can only flush in foreground on reconnect), a ~7-day storage eviction risk for browser-tab (not home-screen-installed) PWAs that can silently wipe queued writes, and no idempotency keys on queued writes (meaning retries after flaky reconnects would create duplicate transactions). All three are now specified: foreground-flush-on-reconnect as the explicit mechanism, home-screen-install prompting + `navigator.storage.persist()` as a supported path rather than optional polish, and client-generated idempotency keys with server-side upsert.
- **Also added**: a "last synced ⟨time⟩" staleness badge on cached reads (mirroring the existing "reconnect to run this" state for compute), an explicit statement that cached financial data sits unencrypted in IndexedDB (a conscious threat-model decision, not an omission), Serwist named as the candidate service-worker library (Server Actions can't be service-worker-cached the way GET responses can, so this needs a real fetch-path design), and a note that auth expiry during an offline window must not drop the queue.
- **A subtler fix**: the server-side-compute decision had been framed as "guarantees full mobile parity" — true only while connected, and in tension with the same commit's own offline-tier discussion. Reworded to "full parity when connected," with an explicit sentence naming the tradeoff (one compute implementation, in exchange for new computations requiring connectivity) so it reads as a documented decision rather than an internal contradiction.
- **Terminology fix**: "sync" had drifted into meaning two different things — bank-data refresh (Enable Banking) and the phone-to-backend write-queue flush. Now "bank refresh" and "device sync" throughout, to stop that ambiguity reaching tickets.
- Confirmed clean: the account-scope and household-size resolutions from the prior round were unaffected by this pass's fixes.

## 13. Eighth Fable Pass — Technical Architecture Build-Readiness Review

First pass explicitly scoped to Section 5 as a whole rather than a specific slice — a senior-engineer build-readiness review of the stack, data model, compute model, provider abstraction, auth, testing, and CI/deploy. ~44k tokens, 2 searches, no length cap. Verdict: **conditionally build-ready** — the architectural instincts (local-first, Tailscale boundary, zero-sync-provider-required, server-side compute for mobile parity) were confirmed sound, but four things were under-specified to the point of blocking Phase 0, not just needing polish.

- **No migration tool had ever been named.** "Postgres" plus a conceptual data model, across 8+ phases of schema change against the household's only copy of real financial data, with no stated way to evolve it safely. Fixed: Drizzle ORM + drizzle-kit (plain reviewable SQL migrations, lighter than Prisma), with a deploy runbook (dump → migrate → start) making the safety ordering explicit rather than implied.
- **The compute execution model was actually wrong, not just vague.** "Server Actions/API routes" for a CPU-bound Monte Carlo run would block Node's single event loop for every household member simultaneously, and a synchronous response over a flaky mobile connection risks losing the result outright — a real contradiction with the offline design's own requirement that results be persisted. Fixed: compute → persist → fetch via a `simulation_run` row and a `worker_thread`, which also supplies the "last computed" artifact the offline cache already needed. Added a seeded RNG requirement, previously unstated.
- **Auth was a posture, not a spec**, despite being a Phase 0 deliverable. Added the concrete five-pointer (argon2id hashing, signed HTTP-only session cookie, brute-force lockout, CSRF handling for the routes Server Actions don't auto-cover, and — the sharpest catch — **HTTPS was scheduled in Phase 6 while auth shipped in Phase 0**, meaning session cookies would travel over plain HTTP for five phases. Pulled Tailscale Serve HTTPS forward into Phase 0.
- **The provider abstraction was forcing two different problems into one interface.** OAuth-based Open Banking (consent flows, token refresh, ~90-day reconsent) and API-key market data providers don't share a real shape. Split into `ConnectionProvider` (OB-specific, owns the auth lifecycle) and `AccountDataSource`/`QuoteSource`/`FundamentalsSource` (pure data-fetch, the original pattern) — which also means a failed Phase 5 spike can't invalidate work already built on the data-fetch contracts.
- **Data model fixes**: JSONB for `retirement_scenario` was confirmed as the right call (not over-normalization), but needed a `schema_version` field and per-`person_id` keying rather than duplicating person facts into the blob. Added `NUMERIC` money types (float was never specified — a real risk for a financial engine), a `currency` column, composite/uniqueness indexes on `balance_snapshot`, `ON DELETE RESTRICT` from person to account, and — a genuine P1 gap, not an edge case — nullable `person_id` with household-level fallback ownership for **joint accounts**, which the original one-owner model couldn't express at all for a UK household.
- **Testing strategy was thin for a financial engine.** "Matches a reference calculator" is a calibration gate, not test coverage. Added analytic closed-form cases (zero-volatility must equal deterministic compounding exactly), property-based tests (monotonicity, seed-determinism), an explicit named edge-case list, a convergence check justifying the iteration count, table-driven UK-tax-rule test vectors for the Cash Allocation Advisor, and a versioned `tax_year_config` module so the annual tax-parameter update is one file, not a codebase-wide grep.
- **CI and deployment were unspecified**; observability was judged mostly fine to skip at this scale **except backup verification**, which wasn't being watched at all — an unwatched `pg_dump` failing silently for months was flagged as the single most likely catastrophic failure mode. Added a GitHub Actions CI workflow, the deploy script above, and a visible in-app "last successful backup" indicator plus a quarterly restore test in Phase 0's definition of done.
- **Confirmed sound as originally designed, no changes**: the local-first Docker deployment, Tailscale as the security boundary, the "must work with zero sync providers connected" constraint, server-side compute for mobile parity, and Zustand scoped strictly to UI state.
